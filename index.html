Provider Tax Financing Analysis — Three-Tier Model

All 51 jurisdictions, three analytical lenses: budget dependence (how much non-federal Medicaid relies on provider-tax revenue), rate-based exposure (states above the 3.5% floor), and structural outliers (waiver states, non-percentage structures, no-tax states).

Start Here — What are provider taxes and what changed?

Provider taxes are fees states charge to hospitals, nursing homes, managed care plans (MCOs), and other Medicaid providers. States use the revenue to draw down additional federal matching funds — so a $1 tax can generate $2–$3 in total Medicaid spending. Provider taxes currently fund a median 18% of the non-federal Medicaid share nationally. They are load-bearing: in high-reliance states, cutting a provider tax forces a direct cut to Medicaid or an equivalent general-fund replacement.

The 2025 "One Big Beautiful Bill" changed three things: (1) No new taxes — states cannot create or increase provider taxes, effective July 2025. (2) Phase-down — for expansion states, existing non-exempt taxes above 3.5% of net patient revenue must phase down 0.5 percentage points per year, from 6% (2027) to 3.5% (2032). (3) Waiver tightening — CMS non-uniformity waivers that allowed some states above the 6% ceiling now face new compliance requirements.

Two exemptions: Nursing facility and ICF/IID taxes are entirely exempt from the phase-down. Only hospital, MCO, ambulance, and "other" provider taxes face the new ceiling. States with taxes primarily on exempt classes are NOT exposed even if those rates exceed 3.5%.

CBO estimate: Provider-tax provisions reduce federal Medicaid spending by $226B of the law's $911B total 10-year reduction. Sources: KFF 5 Key Facts · KFF CBO State Allocation · MACPAC provider-tax explainer · Medicaid.gov provider taxes

Executive Interpretation

What this shows

This simulator lets you move through the five-step phase-down schedule to see how exposure compounds as the ceiling drops from 6.0% (2027 baseline) to 3.5% (2032 final). Each bar represents a state's projected annual revenue shortfall at that cap, calculated as the proportional share of their current tax rate that exceeds the ceiling applied to their total provider-tax base.

Key Takeaways

  • Exposure is highly concentrated — the top five states account for more than 60% of national gap at the 3.5% floor.
  • States with MCO taxes are disproportionately exposed because MCO enrollment scales with Medicaid expansion — the same states that expanded most aggressively face the steepest cuts.
  • The 5.5% threshold (2028) eliminates most at-risk states; the cliff is the jump from 4.5% to 3.5% in 2031–2032.

Research Questions

  • Which states are most likely to respond with benefit or eligibility cuts versus finding alternative revenue?
  • Does the phase-down schedule give states adequate time to adjust budget cycles?

Claims to Verify

  • Provider tax rates are as reported to CMS; confirm no states have already lowered rates in anticipation.
  • Gap formula assumes linear interpolation — verify this matches how CMS intends to calculate compliance.

Limitations

This model assumes no behavioral response (states do not raise other revenue or reduce the tax base). Actual impact will differ from this upper-bound estimate.

High-rate + high-budget-dependence states face a compounded political-fiscal challenge

Each dot is a state (teal = scraped primary source, grey = seed estimate). X-axis: max subject tax rate. Y-axis: gap as % of nonfederal spend. Upper-right = hardest to adjust and most exposed.

Executive Interpretation

What this shows

The quadrant separates states by two independent risk dimensions: how far above the cap their tax rate is (political difficulty of adjustment) versus how budget-dependent that gap is (fiscal severity of non-adjustment). A state in the upper-right is simultaneously hard to adjust and fiscally exposed.

Key Takeaways

  • High rate + high budget dependence states face a compounded political-fiscal challenge: cutting the tax is difficult, and not cutting it is expensive.
  • Several states have very high rates but low budget dependence — these states likely have small Medicaid programs relative to their provider tax base and may absorb the cap more easily.
  • States in the lower-right have moderate rates but high dependence — they are closer to compliance but cannot afford to lose the revenue.

Limitations

Budget dependence is measured against nonfederal share, which varies with FMAP. States with very low FMAP (wealthier states) appear less dependent even if the absolute dollar loss is large.

Most exposure concentrates in the 2031–32 cliff, not the gradual 2028–30 ramp

How national provider-tax exposure grows as the cap steps down. The 5.5% cap (2028) affects fewer states; the jump to 3.5% (2032) drives the steepest increase. Bold red line = national total.

Executive Interpretation

What this shows

The timeline reveals that exposure is not linear — it accelerates in the final two years of the phase-down (2031–2032) when the cap drops from 4.5% to 3.5%. States that appear manageable at the 5.5% cap face a sharply different picture by 2032.

Key Takeaways

  • The 2028 cap (5.5%) eliminates most exposure. The policy impact is overwhelmingly concentrated in 2031–2032.
  • States have four years between enactment and the steepest cuts — budget cycles allow for phased adjustment if states begin planning now.
  • The national total gap at full implementation ($226B CBO estimate over 10 years) is consistent with what the phase-down trajectory projects.

Research Questions

  • Which states will face mid-biennium budget shocks versus those whose fiscal years align favorably with the phase-down schedule?
  • How does the phase-down interact with FMAP cliff effects for newly eligible adults?

MCO-class taxes drive exposure in expansion states; hospital taxes set the floor

Stacked bars show each state's gap by provider class. KY's exposure is MCO-class (5.5%), not hospital (2.5%) — seed data overstated hospital exposure. States with hospital-only tax face different adjustment paths than MCO-dominant states.

Executive Interpretation

What this shows

Different provider classes have fundamentally different political economies. Hospitals are large institutions with significant lobbying capacity; MCOs are commercial insurers with contractual relationships that can be renegotiated. Understanding which class drives a state's exposure predicts the path of adjustment.

Key Takeaways

  • MCO-dominated exposure states are more likely to see benefit redesign and managed care contract renegotiation.
  • Hospital-dominated exposure states face direct service delivery pressure — hospitals that lose tax revenue may close units or exit Medicaid.
  • States with mixed exposure require a portfolio approach.

Claims to Verify

  • Gap-by-class values are estimated proportionally from overall tax rates — confirm against direct CMS tax class filings.

Coverage Loss vs. Financing Pressure

Each dot is an expansion state. X-axis: projected 2032 financing gap ($M). Y-axis: modeled Medicaid coverage losses from work requirements. States in the upper-right face compounding pressures.

Executive Interpretation

What this shows

This chart joins two distinct policy levers — provider-tax financing cuts and work-requirement coverage losses — into a single state-level risk picture. The 2025 reconciliation law operates both simultaneously, and their effects compound: states that lose federal revenue from the cap may face additional pressure to implement work requirements to reduce enrollment costs.

Key Takeaways

  • Financing pressure and coverage-loss risk do not perfectly correlate — some states face one severely and the other minimally, which changes the policy response calculus.
  • States in the upper-right quadrant face a dual enrollment-financing squeeze and are most likely to seek federal waiver flexibility.
  • The correlation, while positive, is weaker than intuition suggests — expansion state size (enrollment) drives work-requirement exposure, while tax structure drives financing exposure, and these don't perfectly overlap.

Research Questions

  • Are states with high combined pressure more likely to pursue Section 1115 waivers?
  • Does the correlation strengthen when weighted by FMAP?

Phase 2 Findings: PA Staleness, KY Class Shift, and RI/NV Waiver Risk

Three risk profiles discovered in Phase 2 scraping that are invisible in seed data. Each requires different policy tracking.

Why these three states matter differently

PA — Stale rate, policy-floor risk

PA's rate is sourced from the FY2022-23 bulletin. PA resets rates annually; the current-year rate may differ. The rate as published (5.5%) sits just above the 5.5% first-step cap — a single point of vulnerability at 2028. Verify the current bulletin before citing PA exposure.

KY — Class identity matters

Seed data showed KY hospital at 5.5%. Phase 2 parsing reveals: hospital = 2.5%, MCO = 5.5%. The exposure class is MCO, not hospital — these have different political economies and adjustment paths. MCO taxes are renegotiated in contracts; hospital taxes require direct legislative action.

RI / NV — Waiver states are a compliance-risk storyline, not a phase-down story

RI (13.12%) and NV (7.139%) operate above the 6% ceiling under CMS non-uniformity waivers. The OBBBA's tightened uniformity requirements put their waiver status — not the 3.5% phase-down — as the primary compliance risk. If CMS does not renew their waivers, the required rate cut is far larger than any phase-down step.
